Teaching procurement software to do its own paperwork

Buying teams spend their days on steps that repeat. We designed a source-to-pay platform where agents take that work and humans supervise it — which meant redesigning the unit of work itself, not just the screens around it.

Context

Procurement is a relay race with too many batons

A single purchase moves through requisition, sourcing, negotiation, award, invoice and reconciliation — each step owned by someone different, each handoff a place to stall. Procol builds the software that runs it. Most of that software historically assumed a person would drive every step personally.

The problem

The expensive part wasn't the decisions. It was everything between them.

Buyers weren't slow because sourcing is hard. They were slow because the work between decisions — chasing vendors, re-keying line items, checking a GST number against an invoice, assembling a bill of quantities — ate the day. The judgment calls took minutes; the connective tissue took hours.

If a step is repeatable enough to write a runbook for, it's repeatable enough to hand to an agent.

Constraints

What made this harder than a normal redesign

  • Money moves at the end. An agent that silently gets something wrong costs real rupees, so no step could be a black box.
  • Audit is non-negotiable. Enterprise procurement gets audited; every action needs an attributable trail, whether a human or an agent took it.
  • Trust starts at zero. Buyers are accountable for these decisions. They won't delegate to something they can't inspect.
  • The old workflows still had to work. Existing customers couldn't be forced into an agentic model on day one.

Decisions

Four choices that shaped the product

Decision 01

Supervision, not automation

The agent proposes and executes, but every consequential action surfaces as something a buyer can see, question and reverse. The mental model we designed for is a capable junior who shows their work — not a machine that acts on your behalf invisibly.

Trade-off: slower than full automation, and more UI to build. Worth it, because an agent nobody trusts gets switched off in week two.

Decision 02

The audit trail became a product surface, not a log file

Compliance needed a record anyway. Rather than bury it, we made the trail legible enough that buyers use it to understand what the agent did — the same artefact serving audit and trust at once.

Trade-off: a compliance feature had to carry everyday UX weight, which raised the bar on its design considerably.

Decision 03

Agentic mode sits alongside the manual path

Teams opt in per workflow instead of migrating wholesale, so trust builds on the steps with the least downside first.

Trade-off: two paths to maintain, and a harder information architecture.

Decision 04

Design the six surrounding surfaces as one system

Reverse auction, GST reconciliation, audit trail, vendor onboarding, template creation and bill of quantities were built to share patterns, so the agent behaves recognisably the same in each.

Trade-off: slower to ship the first surface, faster for every one after.

What shipped

Six surfaces around one model

Japanese reverse auction, GST reconciliation, notifications, audit trail, vendor onboarding, template creation and bill of quantities — each shipped against the same interaction grammar so buyers learn the agent once.
