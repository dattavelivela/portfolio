Posha for Commercial Kitchens

Jul 18, 2025 · Datta Velivela

Why I'm writing this

About 2.4 years ago I interviewed with Posha and made it to the final round. I didn't get the role, but the problem stayed with me. Since then I've learned to cook (weekend lunch is my thing now), and I've kept thinking about where Posha could go next.

This note is one idea: taking Posha from family kitchens into commercial ones.

The real problem in commercial kitchens

The hardest part of running a busy kitchen isn't cooking. It's cooking the same dish, the same way, hundreds of times a day, with a team that keeps changing. That's a machine problem.

Three trends make it worse:

  • Most orders now leave the building. The National Restaurant Association's 2025 report found that nearly 75% of restaurant traffic is off-premise (takeout, delivery and drive-thru). These orders lean towards standard menu items made in volume, exactly the kind of repetitive work a machine handles well.
  • Labour is expensive and keeps turning over. The median US cook earns about $17 an hour (BLS, May 2024), and every new hire has to learn the menu before they're reliable.
  • Consistency slips. Fatigue and churn show up as uneven food, complaints and remakes.

Why Posha fits

Posha already does the hard part at home: a camera watches the pan, dispensers add ingredients and spices on time, and a robotic arm stirs a one-pot meal from start to finish. A commercial kitchen needs the same thing, just more often, for longer hours, and across more units.

Where to start: the pilot segment

The best fit is kitchens that cook a high volume of repeat, one-pot dishes such as curries, rice bowls, pasta and stir-fries.

These businesses have already solved the hardest part, which is distribution. They have the orders. What they lack is consistent, affordable cooking capacity.

How the B2B model would work

  1. Partners train Posha on their recipes. The recipe stays the partner's IP. Posha learns how to execute it and doesn't share it.
  2. Posha caches the execution steps. Once a recipe is learned, its steps are stored and replayed instead of being worked out again each time. That keeps compute costs low at volume.
  3. A kitchen intelligence loop improves the food over time:
    • Read reviews and ratings for each dish.
    • Suggest recipe tweaks (for example, less salt in the dal at one location if reviews keep mentioning it).
    • Test each tweak on a small batch before rolling it out to every unit.

This loop is what makes Posha more than a machine. It turns customer feedback into better recipes, something no individual line cook can do at scale.

The economics, honestly

Posha won't replace a whole cook, because someone still has to prep and load ingredients. What it can do is take over the cook-hours spent on repetitive dishes.

For kitchens, Posha would work on a yearly contract, not a one-time sale. The kitchen pays one fee per unit per year, and that fee covers the machine, software, repairs and replacements.

Here's an illustrative example: a station of 4 units saves 4 cook-hours a day.

ItemAssumptionAmount
Labour saved4 hours/day × $17.19/hour × 360 days~$24,750 a year
Yearly contract4 units × $280/month × 12 months~$13,440 a year
Net savingLabour saved minus the contract~$11,310 a year

These numbers are only a starting point. A real pilot would measure actual hours saved, the prep time added, uptime, and what the contract should cost.

What Posha makes

At $280 per unit per month, each unit brings in $3,360 a year. If a machine costs Posha about its home list price of $1,750, the contract pays for it in about 6 months, and the rest of every year is margin for software and service.

Because contracts renew every year, this revenue repeats instead of ending at the sale. Bigger chains running more units per kitchen would push these numbers up.

How the yearly contract could be billed

  • Fixed monthly. The same amount every month for 12 months. Easy to budget.
  • Paid yearly. Pay for the year upfront at a discount.
  • Per dish. A small fee for every dish Posha cooks, with a yearly minimum. Slow month, smaller bill.

Nothing is paid upfront for the machines, so a kitchen can start without tying up cash, and Posha gets revenue it can count on every year.

What's in it for both sides

For restaurant partners

  • Lower cost per meal on their highest-volume dishes
  • The same taste every shift, at every location
  • New locations open without retraining cooks on core recipes
  • Cooks move to menu development and quality instead of repetition

For Posha

  • Recurring revenue from yearly contracts that bundle the machine, software and kitchen intelligence
  • Real-world volume data that also improves the recipe engine for home users
  • A second market that doesn't depend only on consumer adoption

What's next
